## 1. Woods's explicit pattern for \(\mathbb Q(\sqrt{-5})\)

Let

\[
K=\mathbb Q(\sqrt{-5}),
\qquad
\mathcal O_K=\mathbb Z[\sqrt{-5}].
\]

ANT03 states the following behavior for rational primes:

- \(2\) and \(5\) ramify;
- primes
  \[
  p\equiv1,3,7,9\pmod{20}
  \]
  split;
- primes
  \[
  p\equiv11,13,17,19\pmod{20}
  \]
  remain inert.

This is not an isolated lookup table. It is the residue-symbol description of how a rational prime sees the quadratic extension.

## 2. Reduce the defining polynomial modulo \(p\)

The field is generated by a root of

\[
f(X)=X^2+5.
\]

For an odd prime \(p\neq5\), the behavior of \((p)\) in \(\mathcal O_K\) matches the factorization of \(f(X)\) modulo \(p\).

There are three possibilities.

### Split

If \(X^2+5\) has two distinct roots modulo \(p\), then

\[
(p)=\mathfrak p\,\overline{\mathfrak p}.
\]

Equivalently,

\[
-5
\]

is a quadratic residue modulo \(p\).

### Inert

If \(X^2+5\) has no root modulo \(p\), then \((p)\) remains prime.

Equivalently,

\[
-5
\]

is a quadratic nonresidue modulo \(p\).

### Ramified

If the polynomial has a repeated root, then

\[
(p)=\mathfrak p^2.
\]

For quadratic number fields, ramified rational primes are precisely the primes dividing the field discriminant. Here

\[
\operatorname{disc}(K)=-20,
\]

so the ramified primes are exactly \(2\) and \(5\).

## 3. Legendre-symbol formulation

For odd \(p\nmid10\),

\[
p\text{ splits}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\left(\frac{-5}{p}\right)=1,
\]

and

\[
p\text{ is inert}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\left(\frac{-5}{p}\right)=-1.
\]

Since \(4\) is a square modulo odd \(p\), this is equivalently the discriminant symbol

\[
\left(\frac{-20}{p}\right).
\]

So the prime-ideal picture in ANT03 and the Legendre-symbol material referenced as ANT11 are two representations of the same arithmetic information.

## 4. Why the answer is periodic modulo \(20\)

Quadratic reciprocity converts

\[
\left(\frac{-5}{p}\right)
\]

into congruence conditions on \(p\).

For \(p\neq2,5\), the result depends only on the residue class of \(p\) modulo \(20\).

Among the units modulo \(20\),

\[
1,3,7,9,11,13,17,19,
\]

the split classes are

\[
1,3,7,9,
\]

and the inert classes are

\[
11,13,17,19.
\]

Thus the apparently geometric question

> what happens to the lattice ideal \((p)\) after adjoining \(\sqrt{-5}\)?

is equivalent to the finite arithmetic question

> is \(-5\) a square modulo \(p\)?

This is a very good example of algebraic number theory moving among geometry, ring theory, and finite arithmetic without changing the underlying phenomenon.

## 5. Explicit split prime ideals

Suppose

\[
r^2\equiv-5\pmod p.
\]

Then \(X^2+5\) factors modulo \(p\) as

\[
(X-r)(X+r).
\]

The corresponding prime ideals can be written

\[
\mathfrak p=(p,r+\sqrt{-5}),
\]

\[
\overline{\mathfrak p}=(p,r-\sqrt{-5}),
\]

and

\[
(p)=\mathfrak p\,\overline{\mathfrak p}.
\]

This gives an executable construction:

    input: prime p
    solve r² ≡ -5 (mod p)

    no root:
        inert

    one repeated root / p divides discriminant:
        ramified

    two roots ±r:
        split
        build (p, r + √-5) and (p, r - √-5)

For quadratic fields this is a particularly clean bridge from modular square roots to ideal lattices.

## 6. Examples

### \(p=3\)

\[
-5\equiv1\pmod3,
\]

which is a square. So \(3\) splits.

Indeed ANT03 uses the two ideal factors above \(3\) in the refined factorization of \(6\).

### \(p=7\)

\[
-5\equiv2\pmod7,
\]

and

\[
3^2\equiv2\pmod7.
\]

So \(7\) splits.

### \(p=11\)

\[
-5\equiv6\pmod{11}.
\]

No square modulo \(11\) is \(6\), so \(11\) is inert.

### \(p=5\)

The discriminant is divisible by \(5\), so \(5\) ramifies.

The principal ideal \((5)\) is the square of the prime ideal generated by \(\sqrt{-5}\), up to the usual equality of ideals:

\[
(5)=(\sqrt{-5})^2.
\]

## 7. The lattice visualization

For each rational prime \(p\), begin with the sublattice

\[
(p)\subset\mathbb Z[\sqrt{-5}].
\]

Then show one of three events.

### Inert

The ideal remains one prime lattice:

\[
(p)\text{ prime}.
\]

### Split

The lattice factors into two distinct conjugate prime ideals:

\[
(p)=\mathfrak p\overline{\mathfrak p}.
\]

The renderer should show the two factor lattices separately and then their ideal product.

### Ramified

One prime ideal appears twice:

\[
(p)=\mathfrak p^2.
\]

The difference between split and ramified should therefore be visible as

\[
\mathfrak p\neq\overline{\mathfrak p}
\]

versus

\[
\mathfrak p=\overline{\mathfrak p}.
\]

## 8. A residue-class wheel

Because the behavior away from \(2,5\) depends only on \(p\bmod20\), a useful visualization is a \(20\)-position residue wheel.

Mark:

- \(1,3,7,9\): split;
- \(11,13,17,19\): inert;
- \(2,5\): ramified when the residue represents the prime itself;
- non-units modulo \(20\): not possible for primes other than \(2,5\).

Then let primes arrive in order and land on their residue positions.

This makes quadratic reciprocity visible as periodic prime behavior rather than only a symbolic identity.

## 9. General quadratic-field API

For squarefree \(d\), use the actual ring of integers

\[
\mathcal O_{\mathbb Q(\sqrt d)}
\]

and its discriminant \(D\).

For a rational prime \(p\):

\[
\left(\frac{D}{p}\right)
=
\begin{cases}
1 & \text{split},\\
-1 & \text{inert},\\
0 & \text{ramified},
\end{cases}
\]

with the Kronecker symbol handling \(p=2\) uniformly when desired.

Suggested conceptual interface:

    QuadraticField(d)
    RingOfIntegers(field)
    Discriminant(field)
    RationalPrime(p)
    splittingType(field, p)
    primeIdealsAbove(field, p)
    residueSymbol(D, p)

The key invariant is that the symbolic residue result and the actual ideal factorization must agree.
