# Ideals as lattices

This note develops the geometric picture highlighted on Billy Woods's algebraic-number-theory course page: **least common multiples of ideals as intersections of lattices**.

Source and inspiration:

- Billy Woods, *An unorthodox introduction to algebraic number theory*: https://billywoods.net/ANT.html
- Playlist: https://www.youtube.com/playlist?list=PLSibAQEfLnTwq2-zCB-t9v2WvnnVKd0wn

Thank you to Billy Woods.

The statements below include standard algebraic-number-theory background added here. They are not all claims that Woods states verbatim.

## 1. Quadratic orders are rank-two lattices

Let

\[
R=\mathbb Z[\alpha]
\]

with \(\alpha\) quadratic. Additively,

\[
R=\{a+b\alpha:a,b\in\mathbb Z\}
\cong \mathbb Z^2.
\]

After choosing an embedding into \(\mathbb C\), the two basis elements \(1,\alpha\) become two vectors in the plane.

Examples:

\[
\mathbb Z[i]
\]

is the square lattice, while

\[
\mathbb Z[\omega],\qquad \omega=e^{2\pi i/3},
\]

is the triangular lattice.

This is more than a drawing convention. Ring multiplication acts by integral linear maps on the additive lattice.

For example, multiplication by

\[
z=a+bi\in\mathbb Z[i]
\]

is represented in the basis \(1,i\) by

\[
M_z=
\begin{pmatrix}
a & -b\\
b & a
\end{pmatrix}.
\]

Its determinant is

\[
\det M_z=a^2+b^2=N(z).
\]

So the algebraic norm is simultaneously the area-scaling factor of the corresponding lattice map.

That equality should be an executable invariant in any implementation.

## 2. Principal ideals are multiplication images

The principal ideal generated by \(\alpha\) is

\[
(\alpha)=\alpha R.
\]

Geometrically, it is the image of the ambient lattice under multiplication by \(\alpha\).

For \(\mathbb Z[i]\),

\[
(a+bi)\mathbb Z[i]
\]

is therefore a rotated and rescaled sublattice.

Its index is

\[
[\mathbb Z[i]:(a+bi)]
=
|N(a+bi)|
=
a^2+b^2.
\]

This gives three views of the same datum:

\[
\text{norm}
=
\text{determinant}
=
\text{lattice index}.
\]

For general number fields the corresponding statement becomes the relation between ideal norm and covolume under the Minkowski embedding.

## 3. Nonprincipal ideals need more than one generator

In a ring such as

\[
\mathbb Z[\sqrt{-5}],
\]

not every ideal is generated by a single element.

A typical ideal is written

\[
(a,b+\sqrt{-5}).
\]

Additively this is still a rank-two lattice. The failure of principality means that the lattice cannot be obtained as one scalar-multiplication image of the whole ring.

That distinction is geometric enough to display:

- **principal ideal:** one multiplication map produces the sublattice;
- **nonprincipal ideal:** the sublattice exists, but no ring element produces it as the image of the entire ambient lattice.

This is a better visual explanation of class groups than merely saying "some ideals are not principal."

## 4. Inclusion reverses divisibility intuition

For integers,

\[
(6)\subseteq(3)
\]

because every multiple of \(6\) is a multiple of \(3\).

So a "larger" divisor corresponds to a **smaller** lattice.

For principal ideals in \(\mathbb Z\),

\[
(a)\subseteq(b)
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
b\mid a.
\]

This order reversal is essential when drawing ideal diagrams.

## 5. GCD and LCM of ideals

For ordinary integers,

\[
(a)+(b)=(\gcd(a,b))
\]

and

\[
(a)\cap(b)=(\operatorname{lcm}(a,b)).
\]

This is exactly the geometric picture Woods advertises.

The **sum** of two ideal lattices is the smallest ideal containing both:

\[
I+J=\{x+y:x\in I,\ y\in J\}.
\]

The **intersection**

\[
I\cap J
\]

is the largest ideal contained in both.

Thus, in the divisibility order,

\[
I+J
\]

behaves like a gcd and

\[
I\cap J
\]

behaves like an lcm.

For example in \(\mathbb Z\),

\[
(4)+(6)=(2),
\]

while

\[
(4)\cap(6)=(12).
\]

A renderer should animate this directly:

1. draw the points of \((4)\);
2. draw the points of \((6)\);
3. highlight their union only as a visual aid;
4. compute the subgroup generated by both to obtain \((2)\);
5. separately highlight the common points to obtain \((12)\).

The union itself is generally **not** an ideal. That is worth showing explicitly.

## 6. Product of ideals is not geometric intersection

The product

\[
IJ
\]

is generated by all products \(xy\) with \(x\in I\), \(y\in J\).

For principal ideals,

\[
(a)(b)=(ab).
\]

The product is therefore multiplicative arithmetic, not set intersection.

This should remain a separate primitive in code:

    sum(I,J)
    intersection(I,J)
    product(I,J)

Do not overload one lattice operation to represent another.

## 7. Prime factorization moves from elements to ideals

The classical failure

\[
6=2\cdot3=(1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5})
\]

in \(\mathbb Z[\sqrt{-5}]\) shows failure of unique factorization of **elements**.

The repair is not to pretend that one of those factorizations was wrong. The repair is to factor the associated principal ideals into prime ideals.

The deeper prime-ideal factorization can refine apparently different element factorizations into a common structure.

Geometrically:

    element factorization
        ↓
    principal ideal
        ↓
    lattice
        ↓
    prime-ideal sublattices

This is a natural animation target.

## 8. Class groups as failure of scalar generation

Fractional ideals are equivalent when they differ by multiplication by a nonzero field element.

The ideal class group is

\[
\operatorname{Cl}(R)
=
\{\text{fractional ideals}\}/\{\text{principal fractional ideals}\}.
\]

A geometric reading is:

> classify ideal lattices up to scalar multiplication by field elements.

The identity class consists of principal ideals.

So class number \(1\) means every ideal lattice is, after the appropriate arithmetic notion of scaling, generated by one element.

This wording makes the class group look less like an arbitrary quotient and more like a moduli problem for arithmetic lattices.

## 9. Rendering plan

A first renderer does not need general number fields.

Start with quadratic rings:

\[
\mathbb Z[i],
\quad
\mathbb Z[\omega],
\quad
\mathbb Z[\sqrt{-5}].
\]

Represent a rank-two lattice by a \(2\times2\) integer/rational basis matrix relative to the ring basis.

For each ideal, show:

- two basis vectors;
- a finite viewport of lattice points;
- determinant/index;
- generators as ring elements;
- whether a single generator is known;
- sum/intersection/product with another ideal.

The decisive tests are mathematical, not cosmetic.

For a principal Gaussian ideal \((a+bi)\):

\[
|\det B|=a^2+b^2.
\]

For finite-index ideals \(I,J\subseteq R\):

\[
I\cap J\subseteq I,\qquad
I\cap J\subseteq J,
\]

and

\[
I\subseteq I+J,\qquad
J\subseteq I+J.
\]

For principal ideals in \(\mathbb Z\):

\[
(a)+(b)=(\gcd(a,b)),
\]

\[
(a)\cap(b)=(\operatorname{lcm}(a,b)).
\]

These invariants should be checked before any graphical polish.

## 10. Generalization

For a number field \(K\) of degree

\[
n=r_1+2r_2,
\]

the Minkowski embedding sends its ring of integers to a lattice in

\[
\mathbb R^{r_1}\times\mathbb C^{r_2}
\cong
\mathbb R^n.
\]

The two-dimensional Woods pictures are therefore not isolated tricks. They are the first visible case of the general geometry of numbers.

That is the route this repository should take:

\[
\text{planar quadratic lattices}
\longrightarrow
\text{ideal arithmetic}
\longrightarrow
\text{Minkowski lattices}
\longrightarrow
\text{class groups and units}.
\]
